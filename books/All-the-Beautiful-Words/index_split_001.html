all the beautiful words

 

 

I WAS HAVING a day that nightmares seek refuge from.

It started before dawn, when my phone’s alarm woke me.

I was simmering on my sleeping bag in the heat. It was cooking me raw.

The morning spiraled downward.

Ants had discovered my water bottle, hidden in the canyon facing me. They were inside and out of it, the single oasis in the sands surrounding me.

The descent continued in the soaring sun.

The 111 bus to Cat City was forty minutes late. The 30 bus to Palm Springs and EōS Fitness was later than that.

It didn’t matter. No water was in the gym. None would come.

I had to wash myself in a sink at the Rancho Mirage Public Library.

Slouching in its Quiet Room, I willed myself the wanting to want ahead. I battled to stay upright all day.

The temperature had risen when I left the building at night. A furnace swept across me.

But it wasn’t what would bring me to my knees. Nor was it the scarcity of hope encroaching on me. Or my inability to escape either. Or my self-pity.

A strap of my backpack broke.

Cursing, I dropped to a ground that was burning through my jeans. I tied the strap with safety pins.

They wouldn’t hold.

A drunk bumped into me on the 111 bus back to nowhere. He knocked the strap off before I could get a seat.

“Look where you’re going!” he howled while staggering off the bus.

I began to howl back. But I didn’t finish.

The bus kicked.

A tattered book slid down the aisle. To my toes.

Jiří Orten’s Jeremiah’s Tears.

What would obscure Czech poetry be doing on a bus in the middle of Coachella Valley?

I didn’t care. I vaulted at the volume. At the light that was shining from it. As it had long ago. In a place far away.

 

 

THE SUN LABORED above Prague in August 1942.

My grandpa Hermann woke. In his Ana’s arms.

It made him happy. The happy that got him through each morning since his life had stalled.

But it wasn’t enough to keep him happy.

He eased himself from their featherbed, careful not to wake his wife. He dressed. He had a sense of purpose, having tired of his moping.

Standing in the full-length mirror on the closet door, he put on his favorite clothes. A suit that he had made after the previous war and no longer fit him, due to the beginnings of a belly he’d been developing since before the occupation.

Getting into the suit wasn’t Hermann’s challenge that morning. It was pretending to ignore the yellow star stitched into the jacket.

He overcame it. He left the bedroom. He grabbed his fedora on the way out of his apartment on Eliška Krásnohorská Street.

Hermann was slight, belly and all. He had graying curly brown hair and an unimposing gait. You would never guess that he would’ve evoked fear in those who would induce it.

On this day, no evoking or inducing was going on. It was so early that he was the lone person on the streets in the city’s dwindling Jewish quarter of Josefov.

He made his way to Old Town Square, following a path that he could’ve walked blindfolded. On its ancient cobblestones was a single person other than him. A svelte young man, nearing him from the opposite side.

Wearing a checkered cap, the man was carrying a stack of typed pink papers into the district Hermann had left.

Like the yellow star on his jacket, Hermann pretended to ignore the man’s pink papers. He tipped his hat to the statue of Jan Hus as the man passed.

Hermann reached Celetná Street. He strode to the local police station.

He halted outside it. It was where he no longer worked.

Turning around, he crept back to his moping.

The station door swung open behind him. A voice called out, “Herr Weiss!”

It drew Hermann to Marta, a young woman with light-brown hair. She had the same-colored eyes.

She scampered to him. She hugged him. “How are you?” she whispered in German.

“I’m fine,” he lied in the same language, breaking their embrace.

“We miss you.”

He masked his disbelief. “Yeah?”

“Many of us do. It’s so different without you. The criminals are on the loose.”

A truck interrupted them. It drove by packed with armed Nazi soldiers.

Hermann craned his head at it. “They are indeed on the loose.”

The door to the police station creaked.

Detective Klima, an unkempt, heavyset man a decade younger than Hermann, slithered from it. He evaded Hermann’s sight. “Hey.”

Hermann answered in kind.

Klima swung himself toward Marta. “Let’s go.”

Marta swerved to Hermann. “Another suicide. On Široká. A leaper.”

Hermann shifted in the street’s direction. “I went by it fifteen minutes ago.”

“The call just came in.”

Klima crossed his arms. He scowled at Marta. “Let’s go.”

The two headed off.

Hermann lurched at them. “Mind if I tag along?”

He regretted the words. They stank of desperateness and pitifulness. These were the last he wanted to express. To Klima the most.

Klima groaned. “Go home, Hermann.”

Marta tilted at the detective. “It’s on his way home.”

“Fine. But don’t get in the way.”

Hermann went their way.

The three passed over the cobblestones. They made no sounds but their footsteps. They got to Široká Street.

Down the road, a crime scene waited. A uniformed policeman. A pair of paramedics. An ambulance. A dead woman. She was on a gurney beneath a sheet. Only her shoes stuck out.

The three reached them, drenched from the morning’s heightening humidity and heat. Klima sneered at the paramedics. “You boys got here fast.”

A man with a faint black mustache scratched his head. “We were in the neighborhood when we got the call.”

Klima lifted the sheet.

He didn’t react to the woman’s state. While Hermann couldn’t avoid reacting to it.

“Where did you find her?” Klima snapped at the policeman, his voice assigning fault and blame.

The man trained his reply at the ambulance. “They parked in front of her.”

He whisked his arm toward the building beside it. “Her name was Berta Ohrensteinová. She lived up there.”

Klima pivoted to Marta. He glared. “Write it up for me.”

He didn’t say another word. He lumbered across the street.

On the other side was an unnamed pub. It was not only open at this hour. It hadn’t closed the night before.

Hermann took more interest in the scene than Klima had. His eyes were on the five-story roof that the woman had fallen from. It was a good distance from the street.

Marta skewed her head at him. “What’s wrong?”

“Wrong?”

“You doubt that it was a suicide, don’t you?”

“I still can’t hide myself from you.”

“Why don’t you believe that it was a suicide?”

“I didn’t say that. Or think it. But in my experience, when people leap off buildings, they don’t leap far from them. Most times, they land face-first.”

“Someone pushed her?”

“Either that or she had a running start and somersaulted in the air.”

Marta spied the woman’s heels. “Not in those shoes.”

“You have an excellent mind for this work, Marta. You should be the detective. Klima should be your assistant.”

“I’d rather be your assistant.”

“Oh, well. It was nice seeing you.”

Hermann dithered. But he tipped his hat to Marta. He shuffled off.

The man in the checkered cap cornered onto the upcoming street. The man with the pink papers under his arm. The papers Hermann wished he hadn’t spotted. The papers he didn’t want to approach.

“We could go inside the apartment,” Marta said.

Hermann spun toward her. “What?”

She got the policeman’s notice. “We could, couldn’t we, Pepa?”

“Sure. It’s number 16. On the third floor. Jirka’s going through it.”

Hermann shook his head. “I’m not sure.”

Marta gripped her hips. “You’re sure.”

Hermann’s hesitance lingered. But he trailed Marta into the building. He trailed her up the stairs.

A policeman exited the woman’s apartment.

On Hermann’s ascent, the man stood up straight. He buttoned his jacket. “Herr Weiss.”

Hermann’s face shined. The shine that would welcome me my whole childhood. I saw it on the bus driving across the void. It was sitting me up. It was pushing me beyond my day. “Hello, Jirka. How are you?”

“Just fine, sir.”

Marta swayed toward the door. “Do you mind if we have a look?”

“Klidně,” the man babbled, unable to convey other than in Czech a word that conveyed his assent.

He opened the door. He let the two inside.

Hermann inspected a closet by the foyer. He peeked at Marta. “You’ll have to notify her husband.”

“She had a husband?”

Hermann opened the door in full.

On a rod hung coats belonging to a man and a woman. Their hats were on the shelf above them.

Hermann moved on. To a set of drawers. Marta went into the living room.

He tugged out a passport. He opened it. “His name is Eduard. Ana and I have chatted with him at that café on Maisel. A nice guy.”

“Take a gander at this,” Marta shouted.

She hustled to Hermann. In her hand was a typed pink slip of paper that made him cringe. “The Nazis were sending her to Theresienstadt. She was to leave from Hybernské tomorrow morning.”

“Hybernské?”

“That’s what they’ve renamed Masaryk Station.”

“Where did you get that paper?”

“On a bureau.”

“Was one for Eduard?”

“Nothing but this.”

“Odd. They’ve been sending families to the camps together.”

“He could have his slip with him. He could be at work. Or running an errand.”

“He could.”

Hermann set foot into the living room.

A book was lying flat on a writing desk steps away. Jiří Orten’s Jeremiah’s Tears.

He dashed to it. He picked it up. He flipped its pages. “Ana loves his poems.” He read a couplet aloud.

 

if light is in me

it is also in pain, it’s in you, in you as well!

 

Hermann felt happy. The same happy he had felt in Ana’s arms.

Marta gave the room a scan. “Odd.”

He placed the book on the desk. “What is?”

“It’s odd that a German-speaking, middle-aged Jew would take an interest in Czech modernist poetry.”

“It may surprise you at what a German-speaking, middle-aged Jew would take an interest in.”

“No offense.”

“None taken.”

“It does appear that she killed herself. First, they were sending her to that camp. Then she was reading that book. Leave it to you to select the sole hopeful lines in it. The rest could bring down a city block. Or two.”

“You’re right. It does appear a suicide.”

“But you believe that someone killed her?”

“The totality of my certainty is that she didn’t jump.”

Hermann continued through the apartment. He paused at a window facing Široká Street.

Klima was stammering from the pub. He was returning to Old Town.

Hermann went to Marta. “How are you for time today?”

“I can take as much as I want. Klima must be drunk by now. If I type that report before I go home, it’ll be fine. I’d like to type the truth. It’d be a nice change.”

“Let’s see what we can unravel.”

The two left the apartment. They walked across the street. They walked into the pub, where the stale stench of beer was palpable. Wallowing in it were a half-dozen groggy patrons and an aging bartender.

Hermann explained to them what had happened to Mrs. Ohrensteinová. “Did any of you notice her fall?”

A man raised his hand. He twisted himself toward the bartender. “I told Honza. He called it in.”

“Did you know her?” Hermann asked.

He shook his head.

Another person, tables over, slumped in his seat. “I did. In passing. She was the mother of that poet who got killed.”

Hermann lowered his gaze. “Jiří Orten?”

“That’s him.”

A guy in the room’s far-right corner elevated his brows. “He got killed?”

Hermann sank his gaze lower. “An ambulance hit him by accident. The hospital wouldn’t treat him because he was a Jew.”

“That’s a lot of tragedy for one family.”

Hermann lifted himself up. “Are any of you familiar with her husband? His name is Eduard.”

The bartender drifted Hermann’s way. “He comes in here. A nice guy.”

“When was he here last?”

“A few days ago.”

“Did he ever mention where he works?”

“He lost his job some time ago.”

“When a man loses his job,” interjected a patron, his weariness drilling into his beer, “he loses his purpose.”

Hermann exhaled. “I have one final question. Did anyone exit the building after the woman fell?”

The men swiveled to each other. The bartender bobbed his head at the window. “A back door is in that building. It leads to an alley.”

“Thank you. You’ve been most helpful.”

Hermann and Marta exited the pub. Hermann with a drawn-out moan.

She sighed. “What we learned corroborates that she killed herself.”

Hermann couldn’t prevent a bout of self-doubt, no matter how alien it was to him. “You are right, Marta. I could be wrong about this.”

“It’d be the first.”

“I’ve lost my touch. With my purpose.”

“I don’t believe it. Let’s check that back door.”

“We should check the roof beforehand. We should’ve checked it before the apartment. I have lost my touch.”

“You’re rusty. It’ll wear off.”

The two returned inside the apartment building. They climbed to the roof.

While Marta followed the echo of a door opening and closing in the alley, Hermann faltered to the spot where the woman had fallen.

No sign of foul play was about. But he felt the pull that many have from the edge. Including me. Though mine was fleeting when I got off the bus across the street from the canyons where I camped. I let the story take me onward.

Hermann dipped his head over the precipice, above the chasm below. A force was compelling him farther. It wouldn’t relent. “One step.”

Another of Orten’s verses flashed at him. It was far from hopeful.

 

I’ve found my fall. And on what? Tears!

 

Hermann dragged his left foot out. He would swear that he should’ve plunged.

A smile prevented it.

Ana’s.

It was everywhere. The smile that he had run into twenty years earlier when he stumbled past the doors of U Zlatého Tygra. The smile that had greeted him each morning. The smile that sent him to sleep each night. The smile that said he mattered. The smile that was holding him up.

“Herr Weiss!” Marta wheezed from the roof’s other side. “Quick!”

The shock of her voice sent Hermann teetering.

He regained his balance. He scurried to the ledge facing the alley.

The ambulance from earlier was in the road. Through its open driver’s window, a suited man was speaking to the paramedic with the faint black mustache.

Hermann veered at the man in the suit. “That’s Eduard.”

Eduard doddered. To the vehicle’s back door. He doddered inside it.

Hermann jabbed his finger at the fender. “Do you have a pen to write down that license plate?”

Marta yanked a notepad and a pen from her handbag. She jotted down the number.

The two ran down the stairs. They ran to the rear doors. They burst into the alley. They wheeled toward the ambulance.

It sped from them.

Marta caught her breath. She wiped the dampness from her face. “Now what?”

Hermann needed moments to catch his own breath. He needed further ones to wipe his own face and for his own words to form. “When we got here, the ambulance startled Klima. Why?”

“Much has changed since you left the force. Because getting spare parts is so difficult, fewer ambulances are on the road. But more people are needing them. They must give priority to the living ones. It takes hours before they collect a corpse. Sometimes longer.”

Hermann hoisted his head toward a window above. “A phone was in the apartment. Let’s call the local dispatcher. Let’s learn who was driving that ambulance.”

The two went back to the Ohrensteins’ flat.

Marta made the call. She spoke with someone.

She covered the receiver with her hand. “The woman insists that that ambulance is out of service. She insists that they haven’t sent one here yet.”

“Give her the paramedics’ descriptions. Ask her if she can identify them.”

Marta did.

The dispatcher told her that she worked in a separate office. She couldn’t put a face to a name.

Hermann heaved a breath. “Get the names and addresses of paramedics who aren’t working. Get the reasons they’re not working.”

The dispatcher collected the files of paramedics who worked on different shifts. Or were ill. Or on vacation. They were so plentiful that Hermann wanted to give up. He and Marta wouldn’t have time to call on many.

Instead, he asked for what he hoped would be the smallest and best subset of suspects. Those who had called in sick.

Four people were in the group. But their homes were outside the city center. They were spread out.

Making the task harder for Hermann and Marta was that they had no car. They had to take the city’s rickety and sauna-like network of trams. Adding to their difficulties was the law that had forbidden Jews from riding in trams that had a single car.

It took them two hours to get to the first man.

He lived in a southeastern section called Hostivař. The flu was so racking him that it took him minutes to crawl to the door.

The two had to go west to the river to reach the second man. In Podolí.

He was in worse shape than the previous man. He couldn’t get out of bed.

“Mám takovou kocovinu!” he yelled, apprising them of his hangover.

The third man lived in the city’s northern outskirts. In an area called Libeň.

Hermann took off his jacket as they got to his apartment. He used it to dry his head.

Laying it and the yellow star across his arm, he knocked on the door.

A woman in her thirties answered it.

He showed her his old police ID. “We’re looking for Petr Dobrý.”

“That’s my husband. But he’s not here.”

“We were told that he called in sick today.”

“It’s not that kind of sickness.”

“I don’t understand.”

The woman peered into Hermann. She peered and peered. “Why don’t you come in?”

They did.

Mrs. Dobrá served them lukewarm tea in her living room. Or what was passing for it during the war. “My husband hasn’t been right since the accident.”

Hermann leaned toward the woman. “What kind of accident?”

“He hit someone in his ambulance. He killed him.”

“Did he tell you the name of this someone?”

“It, it was Jiří Orten. It wasn’t Peťa’s fault. They have the drivers driving around like mad these days. It was bound to happen. It’s just a shame that it happened to Jiří. Peťa did what he could to help him. He begged the people at the hospital to take him. And he didn’t know who Jiří was at the time. It was only after he died that Peťa read some of his books. He’s been inconsolable.”

“Does he know Jiří’s parents?”

“He does. They don’t blame him a bit. They hugged Peťa when we went to Jiří’s funeral. But my husband feels guilty. He does what he can to help those people. He’s with them today.”

Hermann slid toward Marta. “Perhaps you could help Mrs. Dobrá clean up?”

The woman shook her head. “That isn’t necessary.”

Marta rose with the tea service, grasping why Hermann wanted her to do it. “It is necessary.”

She and Mrs. Dobrá stepped into the kitchen.

Hermann jumped to his feet. He hurried to a trio of drawers.

On top of it was a photograph. Of a man with a faint black mustache.

Hermann turned over the drawers’ contents.

He got nowhere.

Next, he opened the closet by the front door. He scoured Peťa’s coats and jackets.

In one damp from the past evening’s thunderstorm, he came upon a receipt for a shovel.

Marta and Mrs. Dobrá came back from the kitchen. Marta joined Hermann.

He forced a smile at Mrs. Dobrá. “Thank you for your time. You’ve been most helpful.”

Hermann and Marta departed. They stuttered down the corridor.

Marta snuck up to Hermann. “What did you discover?”

“Where we can discover Mr. Dobrý.”

Hermann said it with heaviness in his voice. The case wouldn’t resolve itself in a neat manner.

The two revisited the trams. They trekked to the city’s Žižkov section. To the New Jewish Cemetery. In the setting sun was the ambulance parked by the entrance.

Inside the gates, a caretaker greeted the two. He told them where Jiří Orten’s grave was.

Peťa was by it. He was swimming in his undershirt, digging a grave. A dug one was beside it. Alongside a cart carrying twin pine coffins.

He halted on Hermann and Marta’s approach. He had been expecting them.

The two stopped beside him.

Peťa swept the wetness, from both his forehead and his faint black mustache. He flicked his wrist toward Jiří’s tombstone. “If Prague has ten thousand more poets, it won’t have another Jiří Orten. And I killed him.”

Hermann held down the sorrow in his throat. “It was an accident, Peťa.”

Peťa resumed his shoveling. He snarled. “Tell that to the beautiful words. Tell it to all the beautiful words he’ll never write.”

“What happened today?”

“I made the Ohrensteins two promises this morning. The first was that I’d give them morphine. To make it easier for them to jump. The problem was that it didn’t help. They couldn’t do it.”

“So how did she die?”

“He wouldn’t tell me. But they were arguing before she fell. Maybe she goaded him into pushing her. Maybe it was an accident. It doesn’t seem to matter.”

“How did he die?”

“When I found him in the ambulance after I parked here, empty bottles were on the floor. But if you ask me, he died of a broken heart.”

“What was the second promise you made?”

“To bury them by their boy. That’s all that does matter.”

Hermann had no more to say. He couldn’t have said more if he had. All he could do was put a pebble on Jiří’s tombstone, what we Jews do to show the dead that we remember them.

He and Marta walked off. She pressed her arm against his. “Aren’t we having him arrested?”

“The sole charge would be for befriending Jews.”

“What am I to put in my report?”

“That you were right. It was a suicide.”

Glumness hung over the two. It remained as they made their way to Old Town. Quiet was the extent that passed between them.

They didn’t speak till they said their goodbyes. They said them at the door that they had earlier said their hellos. They hugged as they had earlier.

Hermann went home. He crossed the ancient cobblestones of Old Town Square. He tipped his hat to Jan Hus.

He had to flail himself forward.

The beautiful words and their silence were screaming in his head. They got so loud that he expected them to bring him to his knees.

Keeping him on his feet was a shimmer. It came from the kindness and selflessness of those who should’ve had neither that day. It was a beacon the Nazis couldn’t douse.

No one could.

Hermann made it to his apartment. The shimmer blazed into the light in his pain.

Ana was waiting for him in her rocking chair. She was smiling. The smile that had refused to let him fall.

It wiped away Hermann’s gloom. Nothing could bring it back.

Even when a slip of pink paper slid under their door.
